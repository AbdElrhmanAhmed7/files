--- CHAPTER 1: A DREAM OF ADVENTURE ---

◆ Comprehension

[CH1-Q1] ANSWER: Malone asks Mr. McArdle for a dangerous assignment because he wants to prove that he is brave and to give his safe, ordinary life a real purpose. Gladys has just refused his marriage proposal, saying she could only love a man who has faced danger, so Malone is looking for adventure, a test of courage, and a difficult story worthy of a brave man.

[CH1-Q2] ANSWER: Malone wanted a dangerous, out-of-the-ordinary challenge — a real test of courage that would take him out of safe, everyday London life, such as a risky assignment in some unknown or troubled part of the world. He did not want another ordinary newspaper task; he wanted something that would make him prove himself as a brave man.

[CH1-Q3] ANSWER: Mr. McArdle was the experienced news editor of the Daily Gazette, the London paper where Malone worked as a young reporter. He was a kind, shrewd older man who listened to Malone's request for a dangerous assignment and pointed him toward Professor Challenger and his strange South American story.

[CH1-Q4] ANSWER: At the beginning of the chapter Malone is a twenty-three-year-old reporter on the Daily Gazette in London. His life is safe, comfortable and ordinary: he writes for the paper, enjoys his friends at the Savage Club, and is in love with Gladys Hungerton. Yet he is not satisfied with it — he longs for adventure and feels his life needs a greater purpose.

[CH1-Q5] ANSWER: Gladys refuses Malone because she says she could only love a brave man — one who has faced danger, travelled into the unknown and done something great to prove his courage. She wants a man whose courage is part of his character, not something acted out to win admiration; Malone, with his safe London life, has not yet earned her love.

[CH1-Q6] ANSWER: By true courage Gladys means bravery that comes from character: a truly brave man acts because courage is part of what he is — he faces danger and does great deeds for their own sake, not to impress anyone. To her, a deed done only to win a woman's approval is not real courage.

[CH1-Q7] ANSWER: Mr. McArdle tells Malone that Professor Challenger is a famous zoologist who has recently returned from South America with an extraordinary story of unknown, possibly prehistoric animals. The Professor refuses to give clear details about what he found or where, and he is violently hostile to journalists, so no one can decide whether he is mistaken or lying. McArdle challenges Malone to find out which it is.

[CH1-Q8] ANSWER: Professor Challenger is famous as a brilliant scientist and zoologist with a great scientific reputation. Many people are interested in his South American story because it claims that unknown animals — creatures science believes extinct — may still be alive, which would be one of the greatest discoveries of the age; yet he gives no clear details and is known to throw journalists out of his house, so curiosity and controversy surround him.

[CH1-Q9] ANSWER: Challenger is brilliant but very difficult. He argues with other scientists, has a furious temper, and physically throws journalists out of his house. In addition, his extraordinary claims are made without clear public proof, so people doubt him and his bad reputation grows.

[CH1-Q10] ANSWER: Challenger refuses to reveal the exact location of his discoveries because he does not trust others and wants to protect his discovery from people who laughed at him and accused him of faking his evidence. This secrecy is understandable, but it also makes many people suspect that he is lying.

[CH1-Q11] ANSWER: Tarp Henry laughs because Malone's plan seems genuinely risky and absurd: Malone knows almost nothing about science, yet he intends to pose as a clever student before one of the most brilliant and bad-tempered scientists in England. Henry finds it funny — and warns him — because he thinks Challenger will see through the trick at once.

[CH1-Q12] ANSWER: Because Challenger hates journalists and would never agree to meet one, Malone writes to him pretending to be an admiring young student who understands and quotes Challenger's scientific work. In this way he flatters the Professor's pride and wins the interview that honest requests could never obtain.

[CH1-Q13] ANSWER: Malone decided not to introduce himself as a journalist because Challenger's hatred of the press was famous — he had insulted and even thrown reporters out of his house. Malone knew that an honest introduction would end the interview before it began, so he hid his real job and posed as a student instead.

[CH1-Q14] ANSWER: With Tarp Henry's help, Malone copies a long, complicated sentence from Challenger's own scientific paper into his letter, as if quoting it with understanding and admiration. Because only a serious student of Challenger's work would quote it this way, the false identity becomes far more believable — and it flatters the Professor to see his words respected.

[CH1-Q15] ANSWER: Challenger, pleased by the letter, replies and grants an appointment at his house in Kensington at eleven o'clock on Wednesday morning. His response finally gives Malone the interview no journalist could get openly, and opens the door to the whole adventure.

[CH1-Q16] ANSWER: Professor Challenger lived in a large house in Kensington, an expensive part of London, together with his wife and servants such as Austin. His study was a scientist's room, with a desk covered with books, maps, scientific papers and fossils.

[CH1-Q17] ANSWER: Mrs. Challenger warns Malone because she knows her husband can become extremely angry and difficult to argue with — "a perfectly impossible person". She kindly advises Malone that if the Professor starts to be abusive, he should simply leave the house at once.

◆ Analysis

[CH1-Q18] ANSWER: Gladys's idea of courage changes Malone's plans completely. She tells him she could only love a man who has faced danger and proved his courage, which makes Malone feel that his safe, ordinary life in London is not enough for him or for her. He decides to leave his comfortable routine and seek a dangerous challenge that will test him and give his life a clearer purpose. The very next day he asks his editor for a dangerous assignment — the first step of his new future.

[CH1-Q19] ANSWER: After Gladys's refusal, everything around Malone suddenly looks small and safe: his newspaper work, his comfortable London life and his daily routine no longer feel like a life worthy of a brave man. His wounded pride mixes with excitement, and he begins to see danger not as something to avoid but as the test he needs. This is why he goes straight to McArdle and asks for the most dangerous assignment the paper can give him.

[CH1-Q20] ANSWER: Through his conversation with Gladys and his visit to McArdle, Malone appears hopeful, bold and a little impulsive. He proposes to Gladys with youthful confidence, and even after her refusal he does not sink into despair — instead he turns disappointment into action. At the Daily Gazette he asks directly for danger, showing a restless desire to prove himself; but his quick decisions and his readiness to use a trick also reveal immaturity beneath the enthusiasm.

[CH1-Q21] ANSWER: Malone's first idea of courage is winning Gladys's admiration through adventure — he links bravery to the reward of her love, something achieved so that people will admire him. Gladys's idea is the opposite: courage is an inner quality, part of a person's character, which shows itself in deeds done for their own sake. Her view is higher and less selfish, and it quietly challenges Malone to grow from wanting to impress into wanting to be worthy.

[CH1-Q22] ANSWER: McArdle's surprised reaction — he says most sensible people want to avoid danger — reveals how unusual Malone's attitude is. Malone actively wants danger, seeing it as a way to test himself, find purpose and prove his courage, while ordinary people see it as something to escape. His eagerness shows boldness and determination, but also a certain immaturity, because he wants risk before he has any real experience of it.

[CH1-Q23] ANSWER: McArdle wants Malone to discover the truth behind Challenger's strange story — whether the Professor is honestly mistaken, deliberately lying, or actually telling the truth about unknown animals in South America. This is an important task because no journalist has yet succeeded with the hostile Professor, and because the story, if true, would be of the greatest scientific and public interest. Success would also make Malone's name as a reporter.

[CH1-Q24] ANSWER: Tarp Henry plays an essential supporting role in Malone's plan. He knows Challenger's world — he explains the Professor's terrifying temper and his record of throwing journalists out — and he supplies the practical means of the deception by helping Malone obtain one of Challenger's scientific papers. Without Henry's advice and the borrowed sentence from that paper, Malone's trick letter would have been far less convincing, and the meeting would probably never have happened.

[CH1-Q25] ANSWER: The ending of the chapter builds suspense layer by layer. Mrs. Challenger's gentle warning makes the reader expect trouble behind the study door, and the deep roar from within confirms that the danger is real. Then the closed door opens to reveal the broad, black-bearded Professor behind a desk covered with books, maps and fossils — a huge, intimidating figure who immediately begins to test Malone. The reader leaves the chapter certain that the meeting will be dramatic and that Malone's adventure has truly begun.

[CH1-Q26] ANSWER: Mrs. Challenger's behavior in the hallway — kind, calm and protective — suggests a wife who cares deeply for her husband but is completely used to his impossible temper. She does not apologize so much as manage: she warns visitors kindly and tries to protect them from his anger. Her behavior implies a patient, understanding woman who knows her husband better than anyone and loves him despite his faults.

[CH1-Q27] ANSWER: The setting of Challenger's study reflects his character exactly. The desk covered with books, maps, scientific papers and fossils shows a serious, hard-working scientist whose whole life is his research. The crowded, overwhelming room also mirrors his big, intense, dominating personality: entering it, Malone feels small and tested. The mixture of learning and disorder suggests both genius and the uncontrolled energy that makes Challenger so difficult.

[CH1-Q28] ANSWER: Challenger's first words to Malone — immediate, technical questions about scientific evidence — reveal a proud, suspicious and dominating personality. He does not welcome his guest politely; he examines him, as if the young man were a specimen, and demands proof of knowledge before granting respect. His attitude shows that he trusts no one and expects to be taken seriously, and it instantly puts Malone's false student identity under pressure.

[CH1-Q29] ANSWER: Malone's desire to prove his courage does affect his judgment, at least partly. His eagerness to be brave makes him rush toward danger without weighing consequences — he adopts a risky false identity, ignores Tarp Henry's laughter and warnings, and walks into Challenger's study despite Mrs. Challenger's advice to leave if the Professor becomes angry. On the other hand, this same desire gives him boldness and energy; his judgment is weakest exactly where his longing to impress Gladys is strongest.

◆ Critical / JRE

[CH1-Q30] ANSWER: Malone's deception is understandable but not fully acceptable, and the chapter deliberately shows both sides. On one hand, honest access was impossible — Challenger threw journalists out of his house — so some trickery seemed the only way to reach the truth. On the other hand, lying to enter a man's home under a false name damages trust, as the fight in Chapter 2 immediately proves. The claim can be made that the importance of the story and the harmlessness of the trick excuse it; yet a method that begins in deceit risks ending in violence and shame. On balance, Malone's purpose was serious, but a fair judgment must admit that his method was wrong even if his aim was not.

[CH1-Q31] ANSWER: Malone's desire for adventure shows both maturity and impulsiveness at the same time. It is mature in the sense that he turns disappointment into action: instead of feeling sorry for himself after Gladys's refusal, he immediately seeks a real challenge that could give his life purpose. It is impulsive because his strongest motive is still to impress Gladys, and he rushes into a risky deception without thinking about the consequences. In the end the two are inseparable — a young man growing up, but not yet grown.

[CH1-Q32] ANSWER: McArdle is both helpful and testing at once. He genuinely helps Malone by giving him the most exciting assignment available — the story of Professor Challenger — and by trusting a very young reporter with a task no senior journalist has managed. At the same time he is testing him: he sends Malone to a man who throws journalists down his steps, and his instruction to find out whether the Professor is "mistaken or lying" is a real professional challenge. A helpful editor, then, but one who makes his reporter earn the story — and the outcome proves he judged Malone well.

[CH1-Q33] ANSWER: Malone's decision to leave his safe life was mostly wise, though it carried real risks. Staying in comfortable routine would have wasted his youth, energy and ambition, while seeking adventure gives his life the purpose and test of courage he craves; only by leaving could he discover what he is capable of. The risk, of course, is that he is young and inexperienced and may be hurt by the dangers he courts. But since the safe life was already making him dissatisfied, the gamble was a reasonable one for a man who wanted his life to matter.

[CH1-Q34] ANSWER: Malone is not truly prepared for the adventure he wants — he is only prepared to want it. He has youth, health, energy and determination, but no experience of real danger, no survival skills, and no knowledge of the science he pretends to understand. His whole method — a trick letter — shows a clever mind that relies on imagination rather than training. The warnings he ignores (Henry's laughter, Mrs. Challenger's advice) suggest that his courage, at this stage, is running ahead of his capability.

[CH1-Q35] ANSWER: Yes, Gladys's definition of courage is convincing. True courage should come from character and responsibility, not from a desire for praise; a deed done only to be admired is performance, not bravery. Her standard — a man who faces danger because courage is part of his nature — matches what the novel itself later rewards in Malone and the explorers. The only weakness in her definition is that she states it as a demand for a suitor, which can sound cold; but as a definition of courage it is sound.

[CH1-Q36] ANSWER: No, Malone is not satisfied with his life at the beginning of the chapter. He has a safe job and a comfortable routine in London, but he feels that his life is ordinary and purposeless, and he longs for adventure and achievement. His dissatisfaction appears in his proposal to Gladys — he wants something great in his life — and, after her refusal, in his immediate request to McArdle for a dangerous assignment. He is a young man hungry for a life larger than the one he has.

[CH1-Q37] ANSWER: Challenger largely deserves his reputation for being difficult, though the reputation is not the whole truth about him. The evidence is clear: his violent temper, his record of throwing journalists out of his house, and the warnings of his own wife and of Tarp Henry all point to a genuinely impossible man. Yet some justice is owed to him as well — his difficulty grew out of years of being mocked and called a liar, and he can show respect to people who deal honestly with him, as he later does with Malone. His reputation is deserved, but it was partly created for him by a mocking world.

[CH1-Q38] ANSWER: Henry's description of Challenger is fairly reliable, and the chapter itself proves it accurate. He warns that Challenger is a scientist of the first rank but violent, quarrelsome and dangerous to journalists — and every element of this is confirmed: Mrs. Challenger warns Malone about her husband's temper, a roar is heard behind the study door, and in Chapter 2 the Professor does bodily attack a journalist. The only limitation is that Henry's account repeats the scientist's public reputation, so it describes the difficult man but not yet the truthful one Malone later discovers underneath.

[CH1-Q39] ANSWER: Challenger's personality at the first meeting is that of a brilliant but arrogant and intimidating man. His physical presence is overwhelming — broad, powerful, black-bearded, behind a desk of books and fossils — and his behavior is a scientist's interrogation: he questions Malone's knowledge at once, demanding proof that the "student" is worth his time. This reveals pride, suspicion of everyone, and a passionate need to be taken seriously. Yet the intensity of his testing also shows a serious man who cares deeply about his work — the same quality that makes him both difficult and formidable.

[CH1-Q40] ANSWER: To prepare honestly for an interview with a difficult scientist, I could first read reliable information about the scientist's work so that my questions were informed, then prepare clear, respectful questions in advance. At the meeting I would introduce myself honestly as a reporter and state the purpose of the interview openly. Finally, I would listen carefully rather than argue, since a difficult man may still respond to fairness and genuine interest. Malone's experience shows that deception only provokes the anger it hoped to avoid.

[CH1-Q41] ANSWER: Behaving honestly matters more than obtaining a story. Reporting is built on trust: a story obtained through deception can misrepresent people, damage reputations and reduce the public's trust in the press as a whole. A lost story can be replaced, but a reporter's integrity, once spent, is very hard to recover — Malone's fight with Challenger in the next chapter shows the immediate cost of deception. The honest journalist may get fewer stories, but the ones he gets are worth printing.

◆ Quotes / MCQ / T-F

[CH1-Q54] ANSWER: The line is Malone's wry admission that adventure is not something one schedules — it arrives the moment one steps toward it. He walked into Challenger's house merely hoping to begin something great, and within minutes he is facing a roaring, intimidating Professor with his disguise already under attack. The sentence closes the chapter with both humor and foreboding: the meeting will be dramatic and difficult right away, not calm or simple, and everything Malone wished for is now rushing toward him faster than he planned.
--- CHAPTER 2: PROFESSOR CHALLENGER'S SECRET ---

◆ Comprehension

[CH2-Q1] ANSWER: Malone had been giving vague, cautious answers like "very interesting" to seem knowledgeable, but when Challenger pressed him to name the specific recent evidence, he realized he had nothing real to say. His hesitation exposed the truth: he had been tricking his way through the conversation from the very beginning.

[CH2-Q2] ANSWER: Malone admits he knows very little about the scientific subject because Challenger's technical questions have caught him out — he cannot name the evidence or keep up the pretence any longer. Confessing his ignorance is more honest than inventing answers, and it is the first step toward the truth-telling that eventually wins Challenger's respect.

[CH2-Q3] ANSWER: Malone was really a young reporter for the Daily Gazette, not the admiring science student he claimed to be in his letter. Once Challenger's questions expose his ignorance, Malone confesses his true identity and job — and the Professor, who hates journalists, immediately explodes with anger.

[CH2-Q4] ANSWER: Challenger became furious because he already hated journalists, believing the press had mocked his earlier scientific claims and called him a liar. When Malone admitted he worked for the Daily Gazette, Challenger felt personally deceived — the young man had entered his home under false pretences. His rage reflected both his wounded pride and his deep distrust of a public that refused to listen fairly.

[CH2-Q5] ANSWER: After ordering Malone out, Challenger advanced on him despite Malone's warning not to touch him. The two men fought, crashed into a chair, and the struggle carried them through the open front door and down onto the steps outside, where they rolled together in front of a policeman and the assembled neighbours.

[CH2-Q6] ANSWER: Malone decided not to press charges because he remembered that he himself had entered the house dishonestly, under a false name, and that his deception had helped cause the fight. Feeling that the fault was partly his own, he told the policeman so and refused to make a complaint against the Professor.

[CH2-Q7] ANSWER: Challenger invites Malone back because the young man's behavior after the fight impresses him: Malone could have had the Professor arrested, but instead he admitted the fault was partly his own and refused to press charges. This honesty turns Challenger's anger into something close to respect, and he takes him back into the house.

[CH2-Q8] ANSWER: The policeman closed his notebook because there was no complaint to record: the injured man refused to make a charge and freely admitted that the fight had been partly his own fault. With no complainant there was no case, so the officer simply left the two gentlemen to settle their differences.

[CH2-Q9] ANSWER: Challenger's theory is that the great cliffs of the isolated plateau cut it off completely from the surrounding world, perhaps for millions of years. Because the plants and animals there lived in isolation, species that died out elsewhere could have survived unchanged — so prehistoric creatures may still be alive on the plateau today.

[CH2-Q10] ANSWER: Challenger set one condition before showing the sketchbook: Malone had to give his word that he would publish nothing about the discovery without the Professor's permission. The condition reflected Challenger's fear of being mocked again by the press, and Malone gave his promise.

[CH2-Q11] ANSWER: One picture in Maple White's sketchbook showed the great red cliffs with a separate rock tower beside them, exactly the place the explorers later find; another showed a strange huge animal with plates along its back that the reference book identified as the prehistoric stegosaurus — a beast that should have been extinct for millions of years.

[CH2-Q12] ANSWER: Challenger opened a scientific reference book on prehistoric animals and placed its picture of a stegosaurus beside Maple White's drawing. The two matched closely, which supported his claim that the sketch was a truthful drawing of a living prehistoric animal rather than an artist's invention.

[CH2-Q13] ANSWER: Most of Challenger's photographs were ruined in a river accident during his return journey, leaving only one blurred, damaged photograph. This weakened his case greatly: clear photographs would have been strong public proof, but a single damaged picture could be dismissed as a fake, giving his enemies an easy reason to doubt him.

[CH2-Q14] ANSWER: Challenger refuses to reveal the exact location of the plateau because after his return to London people laughed at his claims, accused him of faking his evidence, and demanded exact directions as if to trap him. Bitter and distrustful, he now keeps the location secret from everyone — which of course only deepens the mystery around him.

[CH2-Q15] ANSWER: Malone begins to believe because he sees several separate pieces of evidence that all support the same story — the sketchbook with its stegosaurus-like drawing, the damaged photograph showing a winged shape, and the actual pterodactyl wing bone — and because Challenger is so calm, certain and sincere in presenting them. Individually each item could be doubted; together they begin to convince.

[CH2-Q16] ANSWER: Challenger uses the magnifying glass to examine the damaged photograph closely, because its details are too small and blurred for the naked eye. Under the glass, the dark shape on the tower's tree can be seen to be a large winged creature — a detail a forger would be unlikely to invent — so the photograph quietly supports his argument that Maple White photographed a living pterodactyl.

◆ Analysis

[CH2-Q17] ANSWER: The three pieces of evidence support the claim from different directions. The sketchbook shows red cliffs, an isolated rock tower and a creature resembling a stegosaurus; the damaged photograph shows the same cliffs with a strange dark winged shape upon the tower; and the wing bone is a physical bone with dried membrane that matches a pterodactyl. Alone, each could be doubted or explained away, but together they point at the same reality — a lost world where prehistoric creatures still live.

[CH2-Q18] ANSWER: Challenger's personality works against his own truth. His temper and pride make him answer mockery with anger and refusal instead of calmly presenting his proof, so people judge the man rather than examine the evidence. A rude, violent scientist sounds unstable, and an unstable man is easy to call a liar. The tragedy is that the story may be true while the messenger makes it unbelievable.

[CH2-Q19] ANSWER: At the start of the meeting Malone is openly sceptical — he says a man can draw anything and suggests the creature is a bird or a bat. But as Challenger calmly produces the sketchbook, the photograph, the wing bone and the theory of an isolated plateau, doubt gives way to surprise; by the end of the chapter Malone admits he is beginning to believe, and the London street outside even feels unreal to him.

[CH2-Q20] ANSWER: The evidence is stronger together because each item covers the others' weaknesses. Reason: He never avoids danger — he faces it as part of the expedition's work — but he always manages it: choosing when to act, keeping means of protection ready, and withdrawing the group when the odds become impossible; courage as discipline rather than recklessness.
Evidence: At the pterodactyl swamp he does not stand and fight hundreds of creatures — he covers the retreat with his rifle and gets the men out alive; at the camp he organizes watches, keeps his weapon ready and studies the signs before moving; at the bridge he crossed last and calm. Every act is a risk governed, not a risk embraced.

[CH5-Q49] MODEL ANSWER (JRE):
Justification: Scientific discovery and survival danger work together in this chapter to build one picture: a lost world that must be understood because it must be survived.
Reason: Every danger the men face is also information — the attack teaches them the pterodactyls' numbers and temper; the torn plant-eater teaches them a predator hunts nearby; the watcher's signs teach them intelligence lives on the plateau; and every discovery they make changes what they must do to stay alive.
Evidence: The iguanodons prove the theory and simultaneously reveal the plateau's food chain; the pterodactyl swarm gives knowledge and wounds in the same minutes; the smoke by the caves is both the chapter's greatest discovery and its greatest new threat. The lost world is one place — its wonder and its danger are the same thing seen from different sides.

[CH5-Q50] MODEL ANSWER (JRE):
Justification: Lord John acts more responsibly than the two professors in responding to the pterodactyl attack.
Reason: Responsibility in danger means protecting people first; the professors respond as scientists — absorbed in the spectacle even while it wounds them — while John responds as the group's guardian: assessing, covering, retreating, and securing the camp afterward.
Evidence: When the flock attacks, it is Lord John's rifle that lets the wounded group escape beneath the trees; afterward he studies the torn plant-eater's signs quietly, keeps his weapon ready, and organizes the fires and the watches that guard the camp. The professors' knowledge is priceless — but in the moment of fangs, the responsible man is the one holding the rifle.
