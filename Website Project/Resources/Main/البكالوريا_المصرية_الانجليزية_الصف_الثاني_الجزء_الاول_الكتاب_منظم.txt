(1) المقدمة | Introduction
==========================================================================================

This introduction presents the theoretical, pedagogical, and
contextual foundations of the Egyptian Baccalaureate (EB) English
program. It outlines the research base that informs the design

of the course, describes the inquiry-driven pedagogy that runs
through every unit, and explains the assessment principles, learner
attributes, and literary resources that shape students’ experience

of the program. Together, these foundations position English not
merely as a subject to be studied but as a sustained practice of

inquiry, communication, and reflection.

Theoretical Foundations

The program is grounded in the premise that language is acquired
most effectively when it is embedded in meaningful, inquiry-driven
contexts rather than taught as an isolated set of rules. This premise
draws on constructivist theories of learning (Vygotsky, 1978;
Bruner, 1961) and sociocultural accounts of language development
(Lantolf & Thorne, 2006), both of which treat language as a tool for
thought and social participation rather than as an object of study in
itself. It also reflects contemporary applied linguistics, which favors
authentic language use over decontextualized grammar instruction
(Ellis, 2003; Nation, 2001).

Building on this foundation, the program adopts principles of
concept-based learning developed by Erickson (2007) and Lanning
(2013). Rather than organizing instruction around discrete rules
and vocabulary lists, the curriculum is structured around powerful,
transferable concepts—among them identity, perspective, and
responsibility — that recur across units and enable students to
construct a progressively deeper understanding across multiple
contexts.

Pedagogical Framework

Inquiry-Based Learning

Inquiry-based learning is the pedagogical cornerstone of the program. Grounded in
Dewey’s (1938) account of experiential education and more recently synthesized

by the International Baccalaureate (2018), this approach positions students as active
constructors of knowledge rather than passive recipients of information. A substantial
body of research associates inquiry-based approaches with greater student engagement,
deeper conceptual understanding, and stronger critical-thinking skills (Hmelo-Silver,
2004; Kuhlthau, Maniotes, & Caspari, 2007).

Each unit opens with a Driving Inquiry Question that frames the learning journey.
These questions are deliberately open-ended, contestable, and grounded in students’
lived experiences so as to generate genuine intellectual engagement rather than
encourage the pursuit of a single correct answer. Representative examples include:

¢ How do our choices shape our future?

¢ What constitutes a strong community?

¢ Can technology enhance human well-being?

¢ Why do people remember the past differently?

Students pursue these questions through integrated reading, listening, speaking, writing,
discussion, research, and reflection—a multimodal approach informed by research on
diverse literacies (The New London Group, 1996; Cope & Kalantzis, 2015).

Concept-Driven Instruction

Concept-driven instruction differs from topic-based or skills-only approaches because
it develops transferable understanding rather than merely accumulating isolated facts
(Erickson, 2007). Learning is organized around key concepts that recur across units,
allowing students to revisit and refine their understanding from multiple perspectives
over time.

This design reflects research on cognitive development and knowledge transfer, which
shows that conceptual understanding — rather than factual recall alone—allows learners
to apply what they know in unfamiliar contexts (Bransford, Brown, & Cocking, 2000;
Perkins & Salomon, 1992).

The JRE+ Inquiry Model

The program employs a distinctive pedagogical framework—the JRE+ Inquiry
Model— which structures learning through four interconnected phases. This

framework synthesizes established approaches to argumentation and inquiry, including
Toulmin’s (1958) model of argument, the inquiry cycle developed by Kuhlthau et al.
(2007), and Schén’s (1983) account of reflective practice.

J —Justification

Learning begins with an authentic problem, situation, or inquiry question that demands
higher-order thinking rather than simple recall (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001). This
phase activates students’ prior knowledge, a practice supported by extensive research
on schema theory and its role in comprehension (Rumelhart, 1980; Anderson &
Pearson, 1984).

Students formulate initial positions, generate questions, and identify underlying
assumptions, establishing a foundation for the investigation that follows. This process
represents an application of Vygotsky’s (1978) concept of the Zone of Proximal
Development, in which learning is scaffolded from what students already know toward
more sophisticated understanding.

R—Reasoning

Students analyze information systematically from multiple sources by comparing
perspectives, identifying patterns, evaluating arguments, and constructing evidence-
based explanations (Facione, 1990; Ennis, 1989). This emphasis reflects research
showing that effective thinkers engage in disciplined inquiry and consider issues from
several angles before reaching conclusions (Paul & Elder, 2006; Kuhn, 1999).

E— Evidence

Students are required to support every claim with reliable evidence drawn from texts,
research, data, or their own investigations. In doing so, they develop information-
literacy skills such as source evaluation, bias identification, and appropriate citation
(ACRL, 2016).

This emphasis responds directly to contemporary concerns about misinformation and
the corresponding need for critical media literacy (Hobbs, 2010; Wineburg, McGrew,
Breakstone, & Ortega, 2016). It equips students to distinguish credible sources from

unreliable ones—a competency essential to responsible citizenship in the digital age.

+ Reflect and Act

The cycle concludes with metacognitive reflection and consideration of real-world
applications, drawing on research demonstrating that reflection enhances the transfer
and depth of learning (Hattie, 2009; Kolb, 1984).

Students are encouraged to consider how their learning might inform meaningful
action within their communities, linking academic study to social responsibility —a
principle central to education for global citizenship (Oxfam, 2015; UNESCO, 2014).

Contextual Relevance

National and Global Contexts

The program operates within a dual contextual framework that addresses both national
educational priorities and global competencies. Learning takes place through authentic
contexts that bridge local and global concerns, consistent with research on culturally
responsive pedagogy (Gay, 2010; Ladson-Billings, 1995).

Students investigate topics related to Egyptian society and cultural identity, sustainable
development, digital citizenship, vocational education, environmental responsibility,
and global citizenship. This integration reflects Egypt’s educational vision while
preparing learners to participate effectively in an interconnected world.

Development of Future Skills

The course intentionally cultivates competencies identified by educational research and
employer feedback as essential for university success and professional achievement
(World Economic Forum, 2020; OECD, 2019), including:

* critical thinking and complex problem-solving
¢ effective oral and written communication

¢ collaboration and teamwork

¢ digital and information literacy

* creativity and adaptability

¢ self-management and lifelong learning

These competencies align with the OECD Learning Compass 2030 (OECD, 2019),
which identifies the knowledge, skills, attitudes, and values students need to thrive and
contribute positively in the future.

Assessment Philosophy

Assessment is integrated into the learning process itself, consistent with research on
formative assessment and its role in promoting learning (Black & Wiliam, 1998; Hattie
& Timperley, 2007). Students complete assessments across all four language skills:

¢ Reading: literary analysis, informational texts, and visual interpretation
¢ Listening: conversations, interviews, presentations, and lectures

¢ Speaking: presentations, collaborative discussions, debates, and inquiry presentations

¢ Writing: reflective writing, analytical responses, opinion essays, reports, and
creative writing

Inquiry tasks require students to investigate, collaborate, analyze, evaluate, create,
and present so that assessment captures both language proficiency and higher-order

thinking.
Reflection and Self-Assessment

At the close of each unit, students complete structured reflection activities. This
practice is supported by research showing that metacognitive awareness improves
learning outcomes (Hattie, 2009; Zimmerman, 2002) and builds the self-regulation
skills essential for lifelong learning (Pintrich, 2000).

Academic Integrity

The program upholds rigorous standards of academic integrity, consistent with
international expectations for higher education and professional practice. Students are
expected to:

¢ produce original work

¢ acknowledge sources appropriately

* respect intellectual property

¢ collaborate ethically

* use artificial intelligence transparently and responsibly when permitted

These expectations reflect broader research on academic integrity and its relationship
to ethical reasoning and professional responsibility (Bertram Gallant, 2008; McCabe,
Butterfield, & Trevino, 2012).

Learner Profile

The program cultivates the following learner attributes, drawn from research on
twenty-first-century competencies and global citizenship education (OECD, 2019;
UNESCO, 2014):

¢ Inquirers—active seekers of knowledge and understanding
¢ Critical Thinkers — individuals who evaluate information and arguments carefully

¢ Effective Communicators —individuals who express ideas clearly and persuasively

¢ Ethical Citizens —individuals who demonstrate integrity and social responsibility

* Responsible Decision-Makers — individuals who make thoughtful and principled
choices

¢ Reflective Learners—learners who demonstrate awareness of their own learning
processes

¢ Collaborative Team Members — individuals who contribute effectively to group work
¢ Creative Problem Solvers—individuals who use innovative approaches to challenges

¢ Lifelong Learners—individuals committed to continuously developing their
knowledge and skills

¢ Proud Egyptians with a Global Perspective —individuals who combine a strong
cultural identity with global engagement

Conclusion

The Egyptian Baccalaureate English program offers a theoretically grounded, research-
informed approach to language education. By integrating inquiry-based learning,
concept-driven instruction, and the systematic development of critical-thinking skills,
it prepares learners for academic success, professional achievement, and responsible
citizenship.

The JRE+ Inquiry Model provides a coherent framework for teaching and learning,
while the program’s dual focus on national and global contexts ensures its relevance to
students’ lives and futures. Through a sustained emphasis on reasoning, evidence, and
reflection, the program develops not only language proficiency but also the intellectual
habits on which lifelong learning depends.

1. Exercise and Physical Activity

Health experts regularly recommend that teenagers do at least

60 minutes of moderate-to-hard physical activity every day.

This doesn’t necessarily mean competitive sport. Activities

such as cycling to school, swimming, or even a brisk walk, all
count towards the daily target. What makes exercise particularly
powerful is its effect on the brain: physical activity causes the
release of endorphins, chemicals that reduce feelings of stress and
anxiety at the same time as boosting mood and self-confidence.

Furthermore, teenagers who exercise regularly tend to sleep more
deeply and report higher levels of concentration during school
hours. Despite these well-documented benefits, a landmark
WHO-led study found that more than 80% of teenagers worldwide
fail to meet the recommended daily activity levels, largely due to
sedentary screen-based habits.

2. Nutrition, Sleep, and Recovery

A balanced diet is widely seen as the foundation of physical and
mental well-being. Nutritionists advise that teenagers consume
a varied range of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, lean proteins,
and healthy fats, while limiting their intake of processed foods,
too much sugar, and high-caffeine drinks. Poor nutrition has
been directly linked to fatigue, difficulty concentrating, and
mood changes, all of which affect academic performance. Sleep
is equally important, but it is often underestimated. Teenagers
require from eight to ten hours of sleep per night to support
brain development, emotional control, and physical recovery.
Research published in the journal Sleep Medicine found that
teenagers who slept fewer than seven hours per night were more
likely to report symptoms of anxiety and depression. Setting up
a regular bedtime routine, such as avoiding screens for one hour
before sleeping, has been shown to improve sleep quality.

◆ الكتابة | Writing

=> How to Structure an Analytical Paragraph

1. Topic Sentence (Claim)
Start with a clear opinion that answers the question.

This sentence should state your position and link to the
question.

Example: Physical activity is more influential than diet in
improving overall well-being.

2. Reason
Explain WHY you believe your claim is well supported.

This sentence should give a clear explanation and show
understanding.

Example: This is because exercise directly affects both
physical health and mental well-being.

3. Evidence

Support your idea using: information from the text, examples
or facts.

Example: According to the text, physical activity releases
endorphins, which reduce stress and improve mood.

4. Explanation
Explain how your evidence supports your claim.
Example: This shows that exercise not only improves
physical health but also has a strong impact on mental
well-being.

5. Concluding Sentence
End by reinforcing your main idea.
Example: Therefore, physical activity plays a more

significant role than diet in shaping overall well-being.
=> Analytical Paragraph Rubric/Checklist

Before you submit your paragraph, check:

- Sentence 1: Clear claim (answers the question)

- Sentence 2: Clear reason (explains why)

- Sentence 3: Evidence from the text (fact or example)

- Sentence 4: Explanation (links evidence to claim)

- Sentence 5: Concluding sentence (restates and strengthens
your idea)

1. The Case for Personal Responsibility
Many health professionals and philosophers argue that personal

responsibility lies at the heart of a well-lived life. From this
perspective, every individual possesses the agency—the capacity

to choose and act, and it is through the exercise of this agency
that identity is formed. Choosing to exercise, to eat nutritiously,
to sleep well, and to manage stress are not simply habits; they
are expressions of who we are and what we value. Psychologists
refer to this as having an “internal locus of control” - a belief
that one’s own actions, rather than external forces, determine
outcomes.

Research often supports the benefits of taking personal
ownership. A landmark study published in the Journal of Health
Psychology found that individuals who scored highly on
self-efficacy measures—that is, those who believed in their
ability to make positive changes—were significantly more likely
to maintain healthy behaviors over time, regardless of their
socioeconomic background.

Furthermore, advocates of personal responsibility argue

that framing health as someone else’s problem can be
disempowering. When individuals attribute their choices
entirely to outside forces, they risk becoming passive. Taking
ownership, by contrast, builds confidence, resilience, and

a sense of purpose, qualities that are central to a positive identity.

. Social and Environmental Influences

However, the idea that individuals are entirely responsible for
their own health is increasingly challenged by social scientists
and public health experts. Research in sociology demonstrates
that where we grow up, how much money our family has,

and the cultural rules we are raised with profoundly shape the
choices available to us.

A teenager growing up in a neighborhood with no safe parks or
affordable sports facilities cannot simply “choose” to exercise
easily. A family with limited income may struggle to prioritize
fresh vegetables over cheaper processed foods. These are not
failures of willpower; they are structural barriers, obstacles
built into the environment that restrict genuine choice.

Moreover, advertising and social media exert enormous pressure
on young people. Brands invest billions in campaigns designed
to shape preferences and habits, often targeting teenagers who
are at a particularly impressionable stage of development.

. The Role of Government and Shared Responsibility

A third perspective argues that responsibility is not an either-or
choice; it does not belong entirely to the individual or entirely
to society, but is shared. Governments, schools, families, and
communities all have a role in creating the conditions that make
healthy living possible and accessible.

Conditional Structures

Academic writing requires precision, caution, and clarity.

¢ Conditional Structures (Hedging)

Conditionals allow writers to express certainty, possibility, or
caution — an essential skill for JRE when making claims based
on evidence.

¢ First conditional (real): “If schools offer counseling, stress
levels may fall.”

¢ Second conditional (hypothetical): “If more schools offered
counseling, students would most likely feel better supported.”

¢ Third conditional (unreal past): “If the study had included
more schools, the findings would have been more conclusive.”

What it means for historical content to “go viral”:

: To “go viral” means to spread rapidly and widely online (often
within 24 hours). This spread is driven by emotional engagement
(surprise, pride, or nostalgia) rather than historical accuracy
or truth.

Never before in human history have historical images, artifacts,
and claims reached many people so quickly. A single post about
an Egyptian pharaoh can accumulate millions of views within
twenty-four hours; a colorized photograph of ancient Alexandria
can be shared across continents before a single historian has had
the opportunity to verify its accuracy. The scale and speed of this
spread are, in themselves, remarkable. The problem is that scale
and speed have no real relationship to truth.

The way social media platforms work increases this risk
considerably. These platforms are designed not to prioritize
accuracy but to promote engagement. Research consistently
shows that engagement is driven by emotion. Content that causes
surprise, pride, nostalgia, or strong reaction spreads more rapidly
than content that is measured, detailed, or carefully qualified. A
viral post may claim that the Great Pyramid was built in a single
decade by a team of fifty engineers. Such posts often accumulate
more shares than carefully sourced corrections. In reality, the actual
construction process unfolded over decades and involved tens of
thousands of workers.

The consequences for our understanding of history are
significant. In Egypt, where the relationship between ancient
heritage and national identity is particularly important, the viral
spread of historical myths and false claims risks distorting the
public’s understanding of who they are and where they come from.

Rarely do virality and accuracy move in the same direction.
Only through deliberate media literacy — the developed habit
of questioning sources, checking dates, and consulting multiple
perspectives — can citizens distinguish between the past that
actually happened and the past that merely trends.

¢ What happens to the jacket price in the example?

One of the most common tricks is called loss aversion. Imagine
a jacket that costs 400 Egyptian pounds. You do not really need
it. Then a sign appears: “Was 800 LE, Now 400 LE, Save 50%!’
Suddenly, the jacket feels much more attractive. Your brain tells
you, ‘If I don’t buy it, I will lose the chance to save 400 pounds.’
Of course, you have not lost anything, but that is how our brains

work. Not buying something feels like a loss.

¢ Name two tricks used in sales.

Sale events also use other tricks. Phrases like ‘Only 3 left in
stock!’ create a feeling of urgency. You feel that you must
decide immediately or you will miss the chance. When you

see many other people buying something, you think it must be

a good idea. And countdown clocks on websites force you to
decide quickly, before you have time to think carefully. All three
tricks work together to push you towards buying.

Why or why not?
Research has found something surprising: people who shop
during sales actually spend more money overall than people who

spent more. The sale is not designed to save you money.

When Places Remember

A historical landscape is more than a collection of old buildings, monuments, and
ruins. It is a place where events from the past have left visible or invisible traces.
A battlefield, an ancient street, a former factory, or even an ordinary house can become
a site of memory when people connect it with important events, experiences, or
individuals. Such places help communities remember their past and consider how it
continues to influence the present.

However, sites of memory do not tell their stories automatically. The meaning of
a place often depends on how it is preserved and presented. A ruined building, for
example, may appear meaningless to a visitor who knows nothing about its history.
Signs, photographs, recorded memories, and personal objects can provide context and
transform a visit to an unfamiliar place into a powerful historical experience. In this way,
interpretation becomes as important as preservation.

This means that museums act as storytellers, but storytelling also involves
responsibility. Museums must decide whose experiences to include and which voices
may have been ignored in the past. Traditional exhibitions often focused on powerful
leaders and major political events. Today, many museums also include the experiences of
workers, women, children, minority communities, and ordinary families. By presenting
different perspectives, museums can offer a more complex understanding of history.

Technology has also changed how historical stories are communicated. Interactive
screens, digital reconstructions, audio recordings, and virtual reality can help visitors
imagine places that no longer exist or events that no longer occur. Nevertheless,
technology should support the historical story rather than distract from it. Impressive
digital effects are of little value if visitors leave without understanding why a place or
object matters.

Ultimately, historical landscapes and museums connect memory with the present.
They remind us that history is not simply a list of dates and events. It consists of human
experiences that can be remembered, interpreted, and sometimes debated. By preserving
significant places and telling their stories carefully, societies can help future generations
understand both where they came from and how the past continues to shape their world.

reduced in many parts of the world. As a result, regions that

Last spring, the Egyptian Youth Environment Network held its

Ms. Hana Karim, opened the event by saying that she had never
seen so much determination from young Egyptians in all her
years of environmental work. She added that the solutions to
Egypt’s environmental challenges were already being developed
by students and young innovators across the country.

One inspiring story came from Omar Taha, an engineering
student from Cairo University. He told the audience that his team
had designed a low-cost solar water heater that could be installed
on ordinary rooftops. He explained that the device could reduce
a family’s electricity bill by up to forty percent. When

a journalist asked him whether the technology was affordable
for lower-income families, Omar replied that the total cost of
materials was less than three thousand Egyptian pounds.

Another memorable moment came from Farida Mustafa,

a sixteen-year-old from Luxor. She said that she had started

a Nile clean-up campaign with just five friends and that it had
grown to over two hundred volunteers within a year. She warned
that the river was changing rapidly and that their generation had
a responsibility to protect it. She encouraged everyone to take
action, however small, in their local community.

As the conference closed, Ms. Karim reminded the delegates
that Egypt was specially positioned to lead environmental
innovation in Africa. She told the young participants that the
decisions their generation made in the coming years would
shape the future of the country and the planet.
